Most voice builds handle one country and quietly break past it — not with an
error, but with a booking that is wrong by some whole number of hours, found by
a person who did not turn up.

The bug is almost never arithmetic. The system has one clock and needs two.

## Two clocks, not one

A booking involves at least two parties who are entitled to see the same
appointment in their own local time, and a business whose availability is
defined in a third. Storing "3pm" is the mistake; 3pm is a rendering, not a
fact. The fact is an instant, and every surface renders it against whichever
zone that surface belongs to.

So I resolve once per lead into an IANA zone — `America/Chicago`, not "CST",
because the abbreviation does not survive daylight saving and the zone does.
Everything downstream reads the resolved zone rather than guessing again.

## Why it is a tool, not a lookup

Resolution runs as a tool the [voice agent](/systems/voice-agents) calls inside
a live turn, which puts it inside a latency budget somebody is listening
through. I built it to about 250 milliseconds average for that reason. A
thorough 900ms resolver would be correct and bad: the caller hears it.

It feeds two consumers:

- **The booking layer**, so the slot written to the calendar is the instant both
  parties meant.
- **The send gate**, so calls and [messages](/systems/messaging-automation) only
  fire inside the *lead's* local business hours. Not the client's. This is the
  half people skip, and it is the half that wakes somebody up.

> A calendar that is right and a dialler that is wrong still rings a stranger's
> phone at two in the morning.

## Tradeoffs

**Resolve early, not per-use.** One resolution at the edge, stored on the
record, read everywhere after. Resolving repeatedly is more "correct" in a
narrow sense and gives every consumer a chance to disagree with the others.

**Prefer explicit over inferred.** Where the form or the number gives the zone
directly, that wins. Inference is a fallback, and a fallback that is wrong is
worse than one that asks.

**Ask when it is ambiguous.** Some numbers do not identify a zone. A caller is
happy to say where they are. They are not happy to be called at dawn.

## Failure modes

Daylight saving transitions, which is why zones are stored rather than offsets.
Half-hour and forty-five-minute offsets, which break code that assumes integer
hours. A lead whose phone number and physical location disagree, which is
common and is why the resolved zone is a record on the contact rather than a
property of the number.

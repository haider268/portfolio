Most people ship an agent and walk away. This is the part that separates a
production system from a demo.

An agent that works in testing works against the conversations you imagined. A
deployed one meets the ones you did not: the caller who answers a question with a
question, the one who says "next Thursday, no wait, the one after", the one whose
first word is "is this a robot".

## The loop

Listen to real calls. Find the turn where it went wrong. Change one thing. Listen
again.

The discipline is in the second step. A call that ended badly usually failed
several turns before it sounded bad, and the failure is rarely the sentence the
agent said. It is the state it was in when it said it.

Failures sort into four buckets, and each has a different fix:

- **Heard wrong.** Speech to text produced something plausible and incorrect.
  Fixed at provider settings, or with a readback on that field.
- **Turn-taking wrong.** The agent spoke over the caller, or left a gap long
  enough that the caller went first. Fixed at endpointing, not in the prompt.
- **Decided wrong.** It had the right information and did the wrong thing. This
  one is the prompt.
- **Was not told.** It needed a fact nobody gave it a tool for. Fixed by adding
  the tool, never by putting the fact in the prompt where it goes stale.

Sorting a failure into the wrong bucket is how prompts grow to two thousand words
trying to fix a microphone problem.

## Tradeoffs

**One change at a time.** Slower, and the only way to know which change did it.

**Tune for the median call, escalate the tail.** There is always a caller the
agent should not be handling. Widening it to cover them makes it worse at the
ninety percent it should be excellent at. That caller wants
[a person](/systems/voice-agents), quickly.

**Latency against accuracy, deliberately.** Endpointing and speech settings are
one dial with a cost at both ends. Set per deployment against real recordings.
